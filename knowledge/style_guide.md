# Deep Tech Sector Briefing — Style Guide

This document defines how sector research briefings should be written and formatted for the Investment Committee at **Northbridge Ventures**, a venture capital firm focused on emerging technology sectors. It exists so the Formatter agent has an explicit, checkable set of rules, and so the Style Reviewer agent has something concrete to grade a draft against — not just a vague sense of "sound professional."

**Context that shapes every choice below:** these reports are read by investment partners deciding whether a sector is worth sourcing deals in, not by policy staff or operators. The reader's actual question, every time, is: *is there a thesis here, is the timing right, and where would we plug capital in if the answer is yes?* Every section exists to answer some piece of that.

---

## 1. Structure (required, in this order)

1. **Title** — framed as the investment question, not the topic.
   - Good: "Should Northbridge Increase Sourcing Activity in Small Modular Reactors?"
   - Bad: "Small Modular Reactor Market Overview"
2. **Executive Summary** (3-5 sentences, no more) — the thesis, stated plainly: is this sector investable right now, why, and at what stage. A partner should be able to read only this and know whether to keep reading or forward it to a specific deal team.
3. **What's Changing** (bulleted, 4-6 bullets) — the specific technical, regulatory, or market shifts that make this sector different *now* than it was 12-24 months ago. Not general background — the delta.
4. **Competitive Landscape: Leaders & New Entrants** — split into two short subsections:
   - *Incumbents / how they're innovating* — established players and what's actually new about their approach, not just "Company X is investing in this space."
   - *New entrants worth watching* — named companies, founding year or funding stage if known, what's differentiated about them.
5. **Where Capital Is Flowing** (bulleted or a short table) — recent funding rounds, which investor types are active (strategic, growth, seed), valuation trends if available, and any notable absence of capital that itself signals something (e.g., "no major growth-stage rounds yet" is a finding, not a gap).
6. **Where Sources Disagree** (only include if genuine tension exists — omit rather than pad) — where internal portfolio/deal-team knowledge and external market research point in different directions. Partners specifically value being told about disagreement rather than having it smoothed into consensus.
7. **Investment Recommendation** (numbered, 2-4 items) — each one a concrete next action: *invest / actively source deals / monitor for N quarters / pass, and why*. Not generic advice — something an investment committee could vote on.
8. **Sources** (appendix, not inline) — full list of internal and external sources. Keep out of the main body.

Do not add a "Background" or "Technology Explainer" section — assume the reader is investment-literate about the general space; if a specific technical detail needs explaining to make a finding land, do it inline in one clause, not a dedicated section.

---

## 2. Tone

- **Decisive, not descriptive.** A sector briefing that only describes the landscape without taking a position on investability has failed at its actual job. Every section should be building toward the Recommendation, not just cataloguing facts.
- **Comfortable with genuine uncertainty, allergic to hedge-everything.** Emerging tech sectors are inherently uncertain — say so plainly where it's true ("commercial timelines here are genuinely unclear before 2030") rather than either overclaiming confidence or drowning every sentence in qualifiers.
- **Named entities over vague categories.** "Several startups are pursuing this" is close to useless to a partner; "Company A (seed, 2023) and Company B (Series A, 2024) are pursuing this with different reactor designs" is usable.
- **No promotional language.** Avoid "revolutionary," "game-changing," "poised to disrupt." Partners read hype-adjacent language as a signal the underlying thesis is weak.
- **Risk-explicit.** Every recommendation should carry its main risk in the same sentence or the next one — regulatory, technical, capital-intensity, timeline — not buried in a separate caveats section nobody reads.

---

## 3. Formatting conventions

- **Bullets over paragraphs** for What's Changing, Competitive Landscape, and Capital Flows. Reserve full paragraphs for the Executive Summary and the reasoning behind each Recommendation.
- **Bold the specific number, date, company name, or funding figure** in each bullet — partners scan for these first.
- **A short table is preferred over bullets for Capital Flows** when there are 3+ funding events to report (columns: Company, Round, Amount, Date, Lead Investor).
- **No more than two levels of nesting** in any bulleted list.
- **Target length: 800-1100 words** in the main body (Executive Summary through Recommendation), excluding the Sources appendix. A briefing that needs to run longer to be honest should say so explicitly rather than silently exceeding this.
- **Markdown headers** (`##`) for each section, exactly as named in Section 1, so briefings are visually consistent across sectors and analysts.

## 5. Common failure modes to avoid (things past drafts have gotten wrong)

- Writing a market overview that never actually commits to an investment position — the Recommendation section existing is not enough if the rest of the report reads as neutral encyclopedia content.
- Naming "several players" or "many startups" instead of specific companies with specific differentiation.
- Treating capital flow data as a list of trivia rather than a signal — a briefing should say what the *pattern* of funding implies (e.g., "seed-heavy, no growth rounds yet" implies a thesis about timing, not just a list of rounds).
- Over-hedging every sentence about technical feasibility, which reads as not having done the diligence rather than as appropriate caution.
- Including citations inline in the body instead of the Sources appendix.
- Treating "Where Sources Disagree" as required — omit it entirely when internal and external views actually align; don't manufacture tension to fill the section.
